Chromosphere Density Check

Is the Fuller & Tsuna (2024) time-averaged chromosphere, built from our MESA red supergiants, denser than the extended atmospheres actually observed around Betelgeuse and Antares? The grain-growth runs suggested it is: in that density, efficient silicate dust formation overproduces the wind by 1 to 3 orders of magnitude.

What holds up Betelgeuse's atmosphere

A steady wind with the observed density (Dent et al. 2024, R* = 1014 Rsun = 7.05e13 cm, 18 Msun, Ṁ = 2e-6 Msun/yr) moves at 0.3–5 km/s inside 30 R*. The force it needs, as a fraction of gravity, follows from the momentum equation with the observed temperature.

  • Radiation on the gas cannot hold up or drive this atmosphere. Something non-radiative (convective or pulsation shocks, Alfvén waves) has to supply pressure equivalent to 10–20 km/s.
  • The Dent profile fits the Fuller & Tsuna form with v_esc/v_con = 10.35 (v_con = 8–9.4 km/s); its escaping tail gives Ṁ = 2.9–4.2e-6 Msun/yr, the observed rate, at 32–37 km/s.
  • In this slow flow, grains seed from 1.7 R* (pure forsterite) to 2.8 R* (about 1% Fe) and reach 0.1 µm by 1.8–5 R*, matching the observed inner dust. A net outward push (Γ_req > 1) is needed only beyond about 12–20 R*, where Γ_dust ≈ 1 is enough; efficient growth gives 3–6 and would overdrive the wind.

Update with the Dent et al. 2026 model

Dent, Richards, Harper, Matthews & O’Gorman 2026 (arXiv:2608.19339, Appendix B) fit a new semi-empirical model to 2021–2023 ALMA and VLA data: a thin chromosphere (scale height 0.06 R*, turbulence 19.5 km/s most-probable) on top of a slowly accelerating wind (Ṁ = 3.8e-6 Msun/yr, v∞ = 9 km/s), with R* = 812 Rsun = 5.65e13 cm at 172 pc, 17.5 Msun, Teff 3650 K. The budget was rerun with these parameters. Dashed lines are the earlier run on the Dent et al. 2024 profile (R* = 1014 Rsun at 222 pc).

  • About 80% of gravity still has to be carried by non-radiative support from 1.15 to 30 R*: v_t ≈ 12 km/s at the chromosphere base, 16–21 km/s at 1.5–5 R*, 7–12 km/s beyond 10 R*. The wind's inertia is negligible (v·dv/dr ≤ 0.05 g).
  • The paper's 0.06 R* scale height (its Eq. 2) is reproduced only with the full gravity 0.73 cm/s², not the reduced 0.32 it quotes; the reduction would mean an effective Γ = 0.56, which our radiation force does not supply.
  • Ballistic parcels launched in this density form no dusty wind (Γ_dust ≤ 1.3). The Great Dimming clump, which formed within months, needs a denser or slower parcel than this mean flow, where growth to 0.1 µm takes about 30 yr.

  • Radius convention matters. Harper, Brown & Lim 2001 use the 11.15 µm diameter (56 mas), 1.32 times the near-IR photospheric diameter (42.5 mas). Their radii are rescaled to the photospheric scale here, which is what the model's R means. Without that rescaling the observed densities would look another 3–4× lower at 2 R*.
  • Distance. Radio free-free opacity scales as n_H² × path length, so n_H ∝ d^(−1/2): moving Betelgeuse from 131 to 222 pc lowers the Harper densities by 0.77.
  • Clumping. Radio data measure ⟨n_H²⟩, so the smooth density they imply is an upper bound on the mean. Clumping makes the model look even denser by comparison.
  • Uncertainty. About a factor of 3 from distance and radius, the metal ionisation fraction (n_H ∝ x_e^(−1/2)) and epoch variability (radio temperatures changed between 2019 and 2026). The Antares MOLsphere value is uncertain by about 10× (geometry and chemistry).

Bottom line

  • The model chromosphere is too dense at 1.5–3 R* by about 3–5× against golden16 as built, and 2–12× when scaled to Betelgeuse, depending on distance and radius. The excess is largest near 2 R*, falls to about 2× at 4–5 R* where the observed profile steepens (∝ r^−2.85), and reaches 30–50× in the Antares MOLsphere near 1.25 R*.
  • The model's mass-loss rate is too high by the same order (5–10×). A 10–25% lower convective velocity, about 6–7 km/s instead of MESA's 8.2, would bring the profile onto the observations, because the density is exponential in v_esc/v_con.
  • The observed mean outflow inside 5 R* is under 5 km/s, so this gas is mostly quasi-static or turbulent.
